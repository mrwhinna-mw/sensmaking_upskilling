Meet the Pod

Effort v. Impact

Some people would engage — if they believed a given civic action was worth the effort. That's not apathy. It's a missing piece of information: what's available to do, and whether it worked. If I go to a protest, what actually happens?

The Paradox

Why this stays stuck

The self-undoing tension

Civic engagement needs mass participation to produce a legitimate outcome — but that same aggregation is exactly what makes it impossible for any one person to see their own causal contribution.

Kept alive by legislative and agency staff who triage input with no mandate to trace it to a result — and by a donor/lobbying channel that gives decision-makers a faster, clearer feedback loop than the public ever gets.

Who benefits: not just distant, unaccountable lobbyists — most local boards and meetings are begging for anyone to show up at all.

Sharpness self-check (pod-declared): 3/3

How we know

The evidence is the warrant

This isn't just our read of the room — it's backed by existing research across three fronts.

Political science

  • The Efficacy Gap (Kalaf-Hughes & Leiter, 2025) — resentment toward institutions drives external efficacy down, independent of whether people trust their own competence to participate.
  • Cross-national data (IJPOR, 46 countries) — low external efficacy doesn't kill participation, it reroutes it "outside the system."

Public opinion

  • Pew: trust in government sits near a 70-year low, with a sharp partisan split — this is the dominant public mood, not a fringe complaint.
  • Pew (2026): the most civically active people are also among the most anxious about whether any of it matters.

Case studies

  • vTaiwan closed the loop on roughly 80% of scoped digital-policy issues — proof attribution is buildable, not just theoretical.
  • Porto Alegre's participatory budgeting made citizen votes show up as literal line items in the budget.

Nine internal extracts back this too — full trail in data/extracts.csv, pod repo.

What we found

The themes beneath it

Theme

What's available

People need to know what civic actions exist and what each one would actually be trying to change. At the local level, that information mostly doesn't exist — so there's nothing to weigh the effort against.

Theme

Whether it worked

Even when someone acts, there's no feedback loop telling them whether it changed anything. Without that signal, there's no way to judge the action worth repeating — or worth trying at all.

The Field

Voices we still need

Where to go deeper before we commit to a frame:

Political science

  • Internal vs. external efficacy — Campbell, Gurin & Miller's original split
  • The Efficacy Gap (Kalaf-Hughes & Leiter, 2025) — resentment, not just structure, drives the drop
  • Cross-national patterning (IJPOR, 46 countries) — education & income effects

Public opinion

  • Pew: trust in government near a 70-year low, sharp partisan split
  • Pew (2026): engagement "types" — who feels efficacious, and why
  • Partnership for Public Service: institutional-performance angle

Case studies

  • Porto Alegre — participatory budgeting with real budget authority
  • vTaiwan — closed the loop on scoped issues, stalled on hard ones
  • Deliberative Polling — clearest paradigm for closing attribution
  • Counterweight: Maji Matone — capacity ≠ willingness to respond
What we're still testing

This is a live inquiry

We formed around evidence, not a brief — so we bring our open questions with us.

1

Unproven audience: we're assuming there are people who'd engage more if the effort-to-impact math improved — untested.

2

Epistemic vs. substantive: maybe people aren't confused about impact — they know they lost, fairly, on substance. Better attribution just delivers bad news faster.

3

Is closing the gap even good? Individual causal weight in a mass democracy really is near zero. Proving that to people could depress participation further, not restore it.
