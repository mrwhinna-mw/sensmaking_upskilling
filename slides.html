Effort v. Impact

There are people who would engage if they felt discrete civic actions were 'worth' the effort they take based on the ability of that action to change outcomes. Its an issue of information about what actions are avalible and access to a feedback loops on impact. If I go to a protest, what happens?

The Paradox

Why this stays stuck

Civic engagement demands that individuals participate at scale so their input can be aggregated into legitimate collective decisions, but that same aggregation — the mechanism that makes participation matter at all — is what makes it impossible for any individual to perceive, or verify, their own causal contribution to the outcome. It's kept alive by legislative and agency gatekeepers (staff who triage input with no capacity or mandate to trace it to a result) and by the donor/lobbying channel that offers decision-makers a clearer, faster, better-attributed feedback loop — so there's never enough institutional pressure to fix the public one, since the people with the most reach to change it are the ones least harmed by it staying broken.

Who benefits from the status quo: Unaccountable actors both public and private, but also this isn't a big scary unknowable lobbist issue, most places for engagment are begging for more folks to show up to board meetings, give comments, etc.

What we found

The themes beneath it

  • ThemeWhat to say- and where to say itUnderneath both patterns is the same unmet human need: people need to see a causal line between their own action and a consequence in the world in order to believe the action is worth taking — and at the local political scale, no infrastructure exists, on either end, to make that causal line visible. The front-end patterns fails to supply "what would matter," the back-end pattern fails to supply "did it matter" — leaving the basic human requirement for legible efficacy structurally unmet, not because people lack the will to act, but because the system provides no evidence either direction.
  • ThemeStructural Opacity Converts Distance Into ThreatWhen citizens cannot see how decisions, timelines, or out-of-jurisdiction elections actually operate, they fill the structural void with assumptions of malice, while those facing public exposure self-censor to protect themselves. This transforms routine civic friction into an unmanageable personal risk and widespread institutional distrust.
  • ThemeUnusable Civic Systems Exhaust Voter BandwidthWhen the state designs electoral mechanics and information environments that demand excessive cognitive labor, everyday citizens cannot decode the process and are forced to abandon their political agency.
  • PatternWhat do I wan to say?People are exposed to information but lack a reliable way to convert it into a defensible opinion — the friction isn't that information is missing, it's that turning available information into a judgment requires either a skill (literacy) or a trustworthy shortcut (deference to experts), and both are unreliable under real conditions.
The Field

Who this touches

Voices we still need: Political science on external efficacy Start with the foundational construct, then the current debate: Political efficacy (Wikipedia overview) — good entry point for the internal/external distinction and its history (Campbell, Gurin, Miller's original 1954 formulation). The Efficacy Gap: Resentment and its Impact on External and Internal Political Efficacy (Kalaf-Hughes & Leiter, 2025) — the most current academic treatment; argues resentment toward institutions is driving external efficacy down independent of internal efficacy, which is the exact split you're interested in. Who Feels They Can Understand and Have an Impact on Political Processes? (Oxford, IJPOR) — cross-national data across 46 countries, 1996–2016, useful if you want the socio-demographic patterning (education, income, gender) rather than just US data. OECD Working Paper No. 46: Political Efficacy and Participation — useful because it's explicitly policy-facing and connects efficacy to institutional design choices, not just psychology. Note one finding worth sitting with: external efficacy predicts conventional participation (voting, rallies) positively but predicts "outside the system" and digital participation negatively — i.e., people who've given up on responsiveness through normal channels don't necessarily disengage, they route around the system instead. That's directly relevant to your paradox framing. Public opinion data Pew: Public Trust in Government, 1958–2025 — the long time series; current trust reading is near the lowest in nearly 70 years of the series, with a sharp partisan split (Democrats' trust at a record low ~9%; Republicans under 50 much higher). Pew: How Americans Engage With News, Politics, Religion and Civic Life (July 2026) and the companion Engagement in Public Life is Linked With Views of Politics, News — brand new (this month), uses cluster analysis to sort people into engagement "types," which could be a useful segmentation if you're trying to figure out who feels efficacious and why. Partnership for Public Service: State of Public Trust in Government 2025 — a practitioner-oriented companion to Pew, more focused on institutional performance than individual psychology. Positive case studies (fixed feedback loops, clear attribution) Porto Alegre, Brazil — the original participatory budgeting case, still the reference point because PB was integrated directly into municipal budget authority rather than being advisory, which is what made attribution legible (line items citizens voted on actually appeared in the budget). Overview via the Decision Lab case study on Rome's PB and the comparative analysis in Public Administration (2025), which is useful precisely because it's comparative — it tells you PB works well in some contexts and not others rather than presenting it as a universal fix. Taiwan's vTaiwan — probably your best "closed loop" example. Born out of the 2014 Sunflower Movement, run on the Polis platform, and by its own accounting produced "decisive government action" in roughly 80% of the ~26 policy issues it processed by 2018. See the Computational Democracy Project case study for the mechanics, but also read the Daily Beast piece calling it a "huge flop" for the counter-narrative — worth having both since the honest picture is "worked well on scoped digital-economy issues, stalled on harder political fights." Deliberative Polling (James Fishkin) — the clearest academic paradigm for closing the attribution gap, because it randomly samples citizens, measures opinion before/after deliberation, and in several cases (UK crime policy 1994, Macau press regulation) governments explicitly adopted the resulting recommendation. Participedia's overview is the best single index of cases; the CGD piece on climate shows a more recent application. For the honest counterweight — where feedback loops were built but didn't hold — look at the Maji Matone project (Tanzania water-service SMS reporting) and the broader "haves and have nots" paper on civic tech and government responsiveness. The core finding across this literature: tech increases government's capacity to respond but does little for its willingness — which tells you the paradox isn't solved by better feedback infrastructure alone, it requires the responding institution to have skin in the game.

What we're still testing

This is a live inquiry

We formed around evidence, not a brief — so we bring our open questions with us.

  • What could break this framing: I am assuming that there are folks out there who want to be more engaged and would given the right effort v. impact cost benefit. That the gap is epistemic, not substantive. I've assumed people feel powerless because they can't verify their causal contribution — an attribution problem. But it's equally plausible people know exactly what happened (their side lost a vote, got outspent, got outvoted) and the real issue is that they lost fair and square, repeatedly, on substance. If that's true, better attribution tools don't fix anything — they just deliver clearer bad news faster. That closing the efficacy gap is good, full stop. I've treated low external efficacy as the disease. But the actual math is that any single person's causal weight in a mass democracy genuinely is close to zero — that's not a distortion, it's correct. Some democratic theory (this is a real fork, not just my hedge) argues legitimacy partly runs on people believing their vote matters more than it marginally does. If I built a perfect attribution system and it told most people "your specific input changed nothing," that could depress participation further rather than restore it. I've been assuming the fix is more truth, without checking whether more truth helps.
